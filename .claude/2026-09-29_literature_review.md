# Literature Review: Venezuelan Immigration and Crime in Peru

## Summary

This review covers the evidence on whether immigration raises crime, with a focus on the Venezuelan exodus and on Peru, which hosts about one million Venezuelan-born residents. The international causal literature finds no effect of immigration on violent crime, and at most modest effects on property crime that depend on migrants' legal status and access to work. In Latin America, the one study with victim nationality finds that violence rose against Venezuelans rather than against natives. Peru's only causal study ends in 2020 and does not examine homicides, extortion, or contract killing (*sicariato*). The methods literature implies that a single-origin shift-share instrument is a continuous-intensity difference-in-differences, that distance to one entry point cannot be recentered in a credible way, and that inference should rest on weak-instrument-robust tests.

## International Evidence Finds Little Effect on Violent Crime

### Violent crime

Instrumental variables (IV) studies across high-income countries find no causal increase in violent crime from immigration. Bell, Fasani, and Machin (2013, 1278) report "no observable impact on violent crime" in England and Wales for either the asylum wave or the wave from the eight 2004 European Union accession countries (A8). Their violent-crime coefficients are 0.026 (0.263) and 0.074 (0.096) (Table 4). In US counties, Spenkuch (2014) estimates an IV violent-crime elasticity of 0.010 (0.154), with a first-stage F of about 74 (Table 5). Chalfin (2014, 3) uses Mexican rainfall shocks and finds "a precisely estimated null effect" (Table 6, 24). For undocumented immigration across US states, Light and Miller (2018) estimate IV effects on homicide of −0.50 and −0.55 (Table 4). The review by Marie and Pinotti (2024, 191) finds shift-share IV estimates "invariably more negative" than ordinary least squares (OLS), and never significant.

### Property crime and robbery

Where effects appear, they concern property crime or robbery, and they vary with local conditions. Bianchi, Buonanno, and Pinotti (2012) find that immigration to Italian provinces raises robberies, with a coefficient of 1.023 (Table 7), and leaves all other crime types unaffected. In the United Kingdom, the asylum wave raised property crime by about 4 percent of the mean, while A8 migrants lowered it (Bell, Fasani, and Machin 2013, 1285). Spenkuch (2014) reports an all-immigrant OLS property-crime elasticity of 0.12 and an IV estimate of 0.108 (0.122). The 0.12 figure is the pooled OLS estimate, not an estimate for Mexicans, as the September 24 scoping report stated. The Mexican fixed-effects OLS elasticity is 0.066 (Table 7). Under a German allocation policy, Piopiunik and Ruhose (2017) find a total-crime elasticity of about 0.49 (Table 2), concentrated in high-unemployment counties (Table 6).

Results for refugee inflows are mixed. Gehrsitz and Ungerer (2022) find a 1.5 percent rise in crime per standard deviation of refugee inflow in Germany, with no effect on street crime (Table 5), and note that police alertness may explain part of it (6). In Turkey, Akbulut-Yüksel et al. (2024) find that Syrian refugees raise provincial crime, using distance to Syrian governorates interacted with annual inflows. Kırdar, López Cruz, and Türküm (2022) instead find that the refugee share lowers crime measured by prison admissions, by 8.1 percent at the 10 percent significance level (Table 3). Özden, Testaverde, and Wagner (2018) find that immigration lowered both property and violent crime in Malaysia (Table 4). Masterson and Yasenov (2021, 1069) find that halting refugee resettlement in the United States had no discernible effect on crime.

The meta-analysis by Ousey and Kubrin (2018) pools 51 US studies. It finds an overall correlation of −0.031 between immigration and crime and a correlation of −0.058 for homicide (Table 2, 70).

### Legal status and access to work

Legal status and access to formal work reduce economically motivated crime among immigrants. Mastrobuoni and Pinotti (2015, 17-18) find that gaining legal status cut recidivism among economically motivated offenders by about 50 percent, with no effect on violent offenders. The effect is concentrated in northern Italy, where formal jobs are more available. In Pinotti's (2017, 30) regression discontinuity on Italy's "click days", legalization lowers crime by 0.6 percentage points on a base of 1.1 percent, and "legal status only affects the former type of offenses," meaning economically motivated ones. Couttenier, Hatte, Thoenig, and Vlachos (2024) study Switzerland, where media coverage of crimes by immigrants was two to five times more likely than for other crimes.

### Reporting and perceptions

Immigration changes crime reporting and fear of crime more than it changes victimization. Nunziata (2015) finds in European victimization surveys that immigration leaves victimization unchanged but raises the share of residents who feel very unsafe (Tables 3 and 8). Legalization under the US Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA) raised crime reporting by applicants from 17 percent to 37 percent (Comino, Mastrobuoni, and Nicolò 2020). In Dallas, an immigration enforcement program raised reports of serious crimes by Hispanic residents by 4 percent, with a zero for murders, which serves as a placebo (Jácome 2022, Table 2). These results imply that police-recorded crime undercounts crimes against irregular migrants, and that homicide is the outcome least exposed to this bias. Marie and Pinotti (2024, 183) note that homicide has "limited underreporting (if any)."

## Latin American Evidence Points to Migrants as Victims

The strongest Latin American study finds that violence near the Colombian border rose against Venezuelans, not against Colombians. Knight and Tribin (2023) instrument Venezuelan settlement with log road distance to the nearest of five border crossings, interacted with the August 2016 reopening. Homicides rise by about 25 percent within 100 miles of a crossing. The estimate for Venezuelan victims is −0.6387 (0.1613) per unit of log distance, against −0.5213 (0.5937) for Colombian victims (Table 2). The paper finds no disproportionate arrests of Venezuelans. It is the closest precedent for an instrument based on distance from an entry point.

Other Latin American studies find no increase in crime from settlement, a rise in property crime along transit routes, or effects mainly on reporting. In Chile, Ajzenman, Domínguez, and Undurraga (2023) find a null effect on victimization using a shift-share instrument (F = 17.35). Crime concern rises, and media over-cover homicides committed by immigrants. In Colombia, Rey, Schulze, and Zakharov (2024) find that municipalities on walkers' transit routes saw robbery rise by 0.19 (Table 1). The victims were both Colombians and Venezuelans, and homicides did not change. Ibáñez et al. (2026) find that Colombia's regularization program raised crime reports by Venezuelans by about 12.5 percent, driven by women (Table 3). Its effect on crimes committed by Venezuelans, −0.151 (0.244), is imprecise, with a first-stage F of 7.7-8.5 (Table 6). The September 24 scoping report cited this line of work as *Journal of Development Economics* (JDE) 151: 102652; that citation belongs to a different paper by Bahar, Ibáñez, and Rozo on the amnesty itself.

Descriptive work in the region shows migrants underrepresented among offenders. Ugarte and Vergara (2023) report that immigrants were 7.9 percent of Chile's population but 5.3 percent of people convicted in 2022, although their share has risen since 2018. For Peru in January 2020, Bahar, Dooley, and Selee (2020) report Venezuelans as 1.3 percent of prisoners, against a population share of 2.9 percent.

## Peruvian Evidence Is Descriptive or Stops in 2020

Only one causal study addresses immigration and crime in Peru, and it ends before the 2021-25 rise in homicides and extortion. Groeger, León-Ciliotta, and Stillman (2024) study 196 provinces. They instrument the log Venezuelan share, taken from registrations for the Temporary Stay Permit (PTP) for 2015-20, with the log 2007 census Venezuelan share interacted with year dummies. The first-stage F is 2,300, reported alongside the labor-market results rather than for the crime sample. Their crime outcomes are log reported violent and non-violent crimes from "administrative data" starting in 2011; the source is not named. The IV estimates are −0.048 (0.031) for violent crime and −0.420 (0.172) for non-violent crime (journal Table 8). By their reading, a doubling of Venezuelans lowers reported non-violent crime by 42 percent. The paper does not study homicides, extortion, sicariato, victim nationality, or any year after 2020.

The other Peruvian studies lack an identification strategy. Castro and Mejía (2020) estimate a fixed-effects panel of 50 districts in Lima and Callao for 2017-18, using DataCrim complaint counts, and find no significant elasticities (Table 3, 28). Bendezú Jiménez, Romero Bendezú, and Chumacero Calle (2024) regress national monthly complaints for 10 crime types on Venezuelan entries for 2018-23 and report an elasticity of 0.1443 (660). That estimate is a correlation. The specification also controls for the number of Venezuelan inmates, which is itself an outcome of Venezuelan crime, and the paper's text and Table 3 disagree on the signs of some coefficients.

Official and descriptive sources show Venezuelans underrepresented among the accused and among prisoners. Freier (2021, 3) reports that Venezuelans were named in 1.45 percent of 2019 police reports, while making up more than 2.5 percent of the population. In December 2021, 1,561 Venezuelans were in prison, or 121 per 100,000 Venezuelans against 264 per 100,000 for the whole prison population (MINJUSDH-INDAGA 2022, 27). By January 2024, Venezuelans were 3.4 percent of 95,007 inmates, and 74.5 percent of them were in pretrial detention (Pérez Guadalupe and Nuñovero 2024, 143, 149). The International Organization for Migration (OIM Perú 2024, 20, 24) notes that official homicide statistics record no nationality for victims or perpetrators, and names this as the main information gap. Venezuelans were 101 of 4,196 people imprisoned for homicide, 6 per 100,000 against 12 per 100,000 for Peruvians (OIM Perú 2024, 23).

The organized-crime evidence is journalistic and official, without causal designs. InSight Crime (2025) dates Tren de Aragua's presence in Peru to 2018. It describes an expansion to Trujillo, Tacna, Arequipa, Chiclayo, and Chimbote, a failure to enter Callao, and a shift of extortion targets from Venezuelan migrants to Peruvians. InSight Crime (2023) documents Peruvian groups that extort under the Tren de Aragua name. Extortion complaints rose from 11.5 to 77.8 per 100,000 between 2019 and 2025, and the official report on that rise contains no nationality data (OBNASEC-INDAGA 2026, 29-30).

Peru's labor-market studies set the context for the economic channel. Boruchowicz, Martinelli, and Parker (2024) use a synthetic control for Lima and find that natives shifted into unskilled jobs. Their footnote 7 warns that there were fewer than 3,000 Venezuelans in Peru in 2007, "thus ... this spatial variation may not be a good predictor." Morales and Pierola (2020) find that formal earnings fall in a reduced form based on the 2007 settlement shares. Vera and Jiménez (2025) find that formal employment falls, using occupation cells. Hammoud-Gallego (2024) finds that Peru's visa requirement of June 15, 2019, raised irregular entry by 38 percentage points.

## Prior Instruments for Venezuelan Location

The most common instrument for Venezuelan location is past settlement interacted with a national shifter, and its first-stage strength varies widely. Groeger et al. (2024) report F = 2,300 at province level in Peru. In Colombia, Bonilla-Mejía et al. (2024) interact the 2005 census share with Venezuelan consumer prices and obtain Kleibergen-Paap (KP) statistics of 50-175. Lebow (2022) interacts the 2005 share with a leave-one-out national inflow and obtains KP = 23.35. Rozo and Vargas (2021) use the 1993 share in a reduced form only.

Distance-based instruments are strong in Colombia and weak in Ecuador. Delgado-Prieto (2024) weights road distance from each Venezuelan state capital to each Colombian department capital by origin shares from the Administrative Registry of Venezuelan Migrants (RAMV). This gives F = 287.1, against 33.7 for the 2005 share (Table 2). Lebow (2022, 14) declines to use border proximity as an instrument, because it "has the potential to be directly affected by economic changes related to the crisis." In Ecuador, distance to the Rumichaca bridge, through which 82 percent of entrants passed, barely predicts location. Olivieri et al. (2020, Table 2) conclude that location is "primarily driven by local economic conditions, rather than point of entry." None of the papers read uses altitude to instrument migrant location, and none uses distance from the Tumbes border complex (CEBAF) for Peru.

## Crime Measurement in Peru Limits What SINADEF and SIDPOL Can Show

Peru's official homicide count comes from police records, and the death registry (SINADEF) is suited to measuring trends rather than levels. The National Institute of Statistics and Informatics (INEI) builds the count from police reports in SIDPOL. It checks them against the Public Ministry, the Institute of Legal Medicine, and SINADEF, and removes duplicates by national ID number, place, date, and hour (INEI 2026, 5-6). The 2025 figure of 3,675 homicides (10.7 per 100,000) is a preliminary SIDPOL-based estimate. Mujica and Campos-Vásquez (2026, 12) write that death records "no son información adecuada ni precisa para medir homicidios" (are neither adequate nor precise for measuring homicides). They accept SINADEF "como un baremo para contrastar tendencias" (as a benchmark to compare trends). Their SINADEF series rises from 678 homicides in 2017 to 2,240 in 2025 (13-14).

SINADEF coverage changes over time and cannot be measured below the department level. Coverage of medically certified deaths rose from 57.65 percent in 2016 to 71.6 percent in 2019, while Lima's coverage fell to 65.8 percent (Vargas-Herrera et al. 2022, 123, 126). The same authors state that coverage cannot be evaluated at district level (128). Sempé et al. (2021) estimate national completeness at 62.9 percent (51-77 percent) and find that regional variation impedes comparisons over space and time. Only 21.5 percent of SINADEF homicides carry an ICD-10 cause code (International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision), and the firearm share rose from 54.7 percent in 2017 to 75.4 percent in 2024 (Hernández-Vásquez et al. 2025).

SIDPOL has a series break in 2025 and incomplete coverage of police units. From 2025 onward, counts use a harmonized extraction that filters duplicates, so year-on-year changes that include 2025 are not comparable with earlier years (OBNASEC-INDAGA 2026, 67). Mujica and Campos-Vásquez (2026, 5) add that SIDPOL covered only part of the country's police units until 2025.

Administrative and census counts of Venezuelans differ in coverage and geographic detail. As of June 2023, the National Superintendency of Migration (Migraciones) had 1,151,561 Venezuelans registered as residents. Of these, 59.64 percent lived in Lima department and 14.16 percent had declared no residence. District detail exists only for Lima and Callao (Migraciones 2023, 24, 29, 38-42). Of all Venezuelan entries from 2016 to June 2023, 50.3 percent occurred in 2018 and 27.26 percent in 2019 (Migraciones 2023, 15). The 2017 census counted 47,481 Venezuela-born residents, and INEI published district counts only for Lima province (INEI 2018, 7, 13-15).

## Methods for a Single-Origin, Geography-Based Design

### A shift-share instrument with one origin is a difference-in-differences

With a single origin country, the shift-share instrument reduces to the initial settlement share, so identification rests on that share being exogenous. Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022, 23) state that "with a single industry the identification assumption reduces to that of conventional difference-in-differences with continuous treatment intensity." Their 2025 guide shows that the single-shift instrument is "perfectly collinear" with the share. It calls the exogenous-shifts approach with too few shifts a "non-starter" (Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel 2025a, WP 8, 21). Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift (2020, 13, 22-23) place identification on the exogeneity of shares with respect to changes in the outcome. They recommend tests of what correlates with the shares, pre-trend tests, and overidentification tests. Adão, Kolesár, and Morales (2019, 34) show that their standard-error correction still over-rejects with very few sectors, so it does not apply with a single shift.

Persistent inflows and persistent local shocks threaten the settlement instrument. Jaeger, Ruist, and Stuhler (2018, 30-31) show that with persistent inflows, the estimate mixes short-run effects with ongoing adjustment to past shocks. Their correction needs changes in the national origin mix, which a single origin cannot provide. Card (2001, 43) warns that persistent local productivity shocks can make earlier settlement shares endogenous. The instrument itself originates with Altonji and Card (1991).

### A distance instrument with one entry point cannot be recentered credibly

Borusyak and Hull (2023) require recentering a formula instrument by its expected value over counterfactual shocks. With one realized entry point, no credible counterfactual exists. Their procedure draws counterfactual shocks, recomputes the instrument, and averages across draws to obtain the expected instrument (2). They add that "unit fixed effects generally purge OVB only when the expected instrument is time-invariant" (14), where OVB is omitted-variable bias. With few shocks they recommend randomization inference (18). Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2025b, 7) call adjusting for the expected instrument "necessary for IV identification." They add that parallel-trends approaches "may be especially useful when there are too few shocks" (3). For Tumbes, the only land route used by most entrants, this literature points to presenting distance as a distance-exposure design with pre-trend tests.

### Continuous treatments need stronger assumptions than binary ones

Comparing districts with different exposure intensities requires a stronger parallel-trends assumption than a binary treatment does. Callaway, Goodman-Bacon, and Sant'Anna (forthcoming, 2) show that comparing effects across exposure levels requires "strong parallel trends." Without untreated units, comparisons with the lowest exposure level mix causal effects with selection bias (Remark 3.1, 14). de Chaisemartin et al. (2026, 19) develop an IV difference-in-differences estimator for continuous instruments that requires units whose instrument does not change. Goldsmith-Pinkham, Hull, and Kolesár (2024, 1) show that with multiple treatments, each coefficient is contaminated by the effects of the others. This matters for event studies with several interacted years.

### Altitude acts through too many channels to be excluded

Altitude shapes climate, crops, and institutions in Peru, which makes it a poor excluded instrument. Dell (2010, 1871) calls elevation "the principal determinant of climate and crop choice in Peru." She controls for it and restricts her boundary sample to segments where elevation is balanced (1864, 1870). Nunn and Puga (2012, 1) observe that because geography is time-invariant and acts through several channels, "disentangling the two channels is difficult."

## An Inference Protocol Supported by the Literature

### Weak-instrument-robust tests should be the headline inference

The weak-instrument literature recommends Anderson-Rubin (AR) tests whatever the first-stage F turns out to be. Andrews, Stock, and Sun (2019, 3) recommend reporting AR confidence intervals with a single instrument "regardless of the value of the first stage F." They also warn that screening specifications on F compounds inferential problems (20-21). Keane and Neal (2023, 9) recommend the AR test "in lieu of the t-test even when the first-stage F-statistic is far above conventional standards." Lee et al. (2022, 5) show that the usual 1.96 critical value is valid only when F exceeds 104.7, and provide adjusted critical values: 3.65 at F = 9, 2.27 at F = 25, and 2.08 at F = 49 (Table 3). With one instrument, the effective F of Montiel Olea and Pflueger (2013, 3) equals the robust F, with a critical value of 23.1 (see also Pflueger and Wang 2015). Young (2022, 5, 21) shows that IV results often depend on single clusters, and recommends delete-one-cluster checks and the bootstrap.

### Clustering at department level needs the wild bootstrap

Peru's 25 departments count as few clusters, so inference at that level requires the wild cluster bootstrap. Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller (2008, 1, 38-39) show that tests over-reject with 5-30 clusters. Roodman et al. (2019, 22-24) implement the wild restricted efficient bootstrap for IV and for the AR test in Stata. MacKinnon, Nielsen, and Webb (2023, 24, 40-42) show that 50 or more clusters do not guarantee reliable inference. They recommend reporting the number of clusters, cluster sizes, and leverage.

### Count outcomes call for levels or Poisson, not log(1+y)

Homicide counts with many zeros should be modeled in levels or with Poisson regression, not with log(1+y) or the inverse hyperbolic sine. Chen and Roth (2024, 1) show that "one can obtain a treatment effect of any magnitude simply by re-scaling the units of Y" when the outcome is transformed this way. The problem extends to continuous treatments (Remark 3, 9). Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML) is consistent when the conditional mean is correctly specified and handles zeros naturally (Santos Silva and Tenreyro 2006, 14). The Stata command `ppmlhdfe` drops districts with zero counts in every period (Correia, Guimarães, and Zylkin 2020, 8). Kwon et al. (2026, 11) introduce an IV-PPML estimator with high-dimensional fixed effects (`ivppmlhdfe`), and state that "bias corrections are therefore necessary for IV-PPML even when they are not needed for PPML."

### Spatial correlation requires Conley errors and unit-root checks

Instruments based on smooth geography need inference that is robust to spatial correlation. The Stata command `acreg` implements Conley-type standard errors for two-stage least squares (2SLS) with fixed effects. In panels, its lag cutoff must be set, or errors are clustered only at the district-year level (Colella et al. 2023, 124). Kelly (2019, 5) proposes spatial-noise placebos, because random permutation destroys the spatial correlation structure. Kelly (2020, 29) recommends adding quadratics in latitude and longitude "as a matter of routine." Müller and Watson (2022, 2023) develop spatial correlation-robust confidence intervals, including for IV and AR tests. Their later work shows that, under spatial unit roots, clustered standard errors "perform very poorly, even when cluster fixed effects are included" (Müller and Watson 2024, 1664). Becker, Boll, and Voth (2026) provide the Stata tools to test for spatial unit roots and to transform the data.

### Exclusion, measurement, and multiple testing each have a standard check

Standard tools exist to bound violations of the exclusion restriction, correct for mismeasured migrant shares, and adjust for many outcomes. Conley, Hansen, and Rossi (2012, 18) propose bounds that show how large a direct effect of the instrument must be before the IV estimate becomes uninformative. van Kippersluis and Rietveld (2018, 319, 322) estimate that direct effect in a subsample with no first stage, assuming it is the same in both groups. Clarke and Matta (2018, 3) implement these methods in Stata. Nevo and Rosen (2012) bound the estimate under sign assumptions on the correlation between the instrument and the error.

For measurement error, Aydemir and Borjas (2011, 9) show that fixed effects absorb much of the true variation in immigrant shares, so measurement error becomes a larger part of what remains. Chalfin and McCrary (2018, 21) combine two mismeasured proxies of the same regressor through IV in each direction. That approach requires the errors in the two proxies to be uncorrelated. Levitt (1998, 72) argues that murder is "likely to be immune from reporting bias," and that reporting comparisons should be made within a city over time.

For multiple testing, Anderson (2008, 1487) recommends pre-specified outcome families, summary indices, and sharpened q-values, based on Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli (2006). Clarke, Romano, and Wolf (2020, 10) implement the Romano-Wolf correction in Stata with IV and clustering options.

## Where This Study Fits

The literature leaves open the effect of Venezuelan settlement on lethal violence in Peru, especially during the 2021-25 rise. Groeger et al. (2024) study provinces and police-reported crime through 2020, with no homicide outcome. Knight and Tribin (2023) and Ajzenman, Domínguez, and Undurraga (2023) study Colombia and Chile. The research agents found no paper that estimates the effect of immigration on extortion in the host country, apart from one working paper known only from a summary. A VoxDevLit review summarizes Guerrero and Sviatschi (2025) as "leveraging the timing of Peru's imposition of migration restrictions on Venezuelan nationals." According to that summary, the paper finds that Tren de Aragua's expansion "led to significant increases in extortion, human trafficking, and sexual exploitation, particularly in areas where migrants concentrated" (VoxDevLit 2025, 10). The full text is not public, and the project should read it before claiming a contribution on extortion.

The closest analogues to the claim that migrants "imported" criminal methods concern deportations and historical migrations rather than a current humanitarian flow. Sviatschi (2022) shows that deportations of gang members from the United States spread gangs in El Salvador. Anelli, Pinotti, and Porreca (2025, 2) study Sicilian mafiosi who fled to the United States. They note that quantitative evidence on moving criminal groups "remains limited to cases where criminal groups move within the same national institutional environment" or to deportations. Cabra-Ruiz, Rozo, and Sviatschi (2025) find more organized-crime events in Venezuelan municipalities with more emigration, which is evidence from the origin side. A district-level test in Peru would need to separate the spread of methods from the nationality of offenders, since native groups have adopted the Tren de Aragua name (InSight Crime 2023).
